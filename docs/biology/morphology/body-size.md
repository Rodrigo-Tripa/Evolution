## 1. Biological Definition of Body Size

### 1.1. What is body size?

In biology, body size is a general term used to describe the overall physical dimensions of an organism. It is an important morphological characteristic because it can influence several biological processes, including metabolism, development, locomotion, reproduction, resource requirements and survival.

However, body size is not a single universally defined physical measurement. It can be represented through different variables, depending on the organism and the scientific question being investigated.

The most common measurements include body length, body mass and body volume. Although these variables are related, they describe different physical properties and should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Body length** describes a linear dimension of the organism, measured between anatomically defined points. It is relatively straightforward to measure, but it does not provide information about the organism's width, thickness or mass.

**Body mass** represents the quantity of matter in the organism. It can be measured using a sufficiently sensitive balance. However, body mass is influenced by physiological conditions, including hydration, nutritional state and the quantity of material present in the digestive tract.

**Body volume** represents the three-dimensional space occupied by the organism. It accounts for length, width and depth, but measuring it accurately is more difficult, especially in small organisms with irregular anatomical shapes.

Consequently, body size should be understood as a multidimensional biological characteristic that can be operationalized through one or more measurable variables.

In insects, body size is also influenced by developmental mechanisms that regulate growth and determine when growth terminates. These mechanisms involve hormonal regulation, cell growth and cell division (Callier & Nijhout, 2013).

### 1.2. Body size in Drosophila melanogaster

*Drosophila melanogaster* is a small dipteran insect whose adult body is externally organized into three principal anatomical regions: the head, thorax and abdomen.

These regions have different structures and biological functions, but together constitute the main body of the adult fly.

The **head** contains the principal sensory structures, including the compound eyes, ocelli and antennae. It also contains the mouthparts, which are involved in feeding.

The **thorax** is the central locomotor region. It supports the wings, halteres and three pairs of legs. It is subdivided into the prothorax, mesothorax and metathorax. The mesothorax is particularly developed and supports the main pair of wings.

The **abdomen** is composed of multiple segments and contains several important internal organs, including parts of the digestive, reproductive and excretory systems. It is a flexible region whose dimensions can change with physiological condition and reproductive state.

These three regions contribute to the overall dimensions of the fly, but they do not necessarily contribute equally to every measurement of body size.

For example, measuring the length of the thorax provides information about a relatively rigid anatomical region, whereas measuring the abdomen introduces additional variability because its shape and dimensions can change.

The anatomical organization described here is based on the anatomical atlas of *Drosophila melanogaster* published by Jürgens, Drechsler and Paululat (2024).

## 2. Anatomical Components

### 2.1. Head

The head is the anterior region of the adult fly and contains the main sensory and feeding structures.

Its external anatomy includes two compound eyes, three ocelli, a pair of antennae and the mouthparts. The head capsule provides the structural support for these organs.

Head dimensions contribute to the overall morphology of the organism. Differences in head width, height or volume can therefore contribute to differences in overall body size.

However, head dimensions alone are unlikely to provide a sufficiently representative measurement of total body size. Two individuals could have similar head dimensions while differing in thorax or abdomen dimensions.

For this reason, head measurements may be useful as additional morphological variables, but should not automatically be considered the primary indicator of body size.

### 2.2. Thorax

The thorax is the central anatomical region of the adult fly and is particularly important for locomotion.

It contains the structures responsible for supporting the wings, halteres and legs. Its external anatomy includes several cuticular structures, particularly the dorsal region known as the notum.

The mesothorax is especially developed and supports the main pair of wings. Its dorsal surface contains the scutum and scutellum.

Thoracic dimensions are particularly useful when studying body size because the thorax provides a relatively well-defined anatomical reference. Thorax length is frequently used in experimental studies of *Drosophila* as a morphological indicator of body size.

Nevertheless, thorax length does not represent the complete dimensions of the organism. It is a proxy for overall body size rather than a direct measurement of total body volume or mass.

For the Genome Evolution Lab, thorax dimensions are a strong candidate for a primary morphological measurement because they are anatomically defined and have been used in experimental research.

### 2.3. Abdomen

The abdomen is the posterior and generally largest of the three main external body regions in *Drosophila melanogaster*.

It contains several internal organs and is divided into a series of visible segments. These segments are connected by flexible regions of cuticle, allowing the abdomen to bend and change shape.

Abdominal dimensions contribute significantly to the overall morphology of the fly. However, they introduce several measurement difficulties.

The abdomen can change its dimensions depending on physiological condition, nutritional state, reproductive condition and the quantity of material inside the digestive tract.

In females, reproductive development and egg production can also influence abdominal dimensions.

Consequently, abdominal length or volume may not always represent stable structural differences between individuals.

For this project, abdominal dimensions should be considered a possible additional morphological variable, but their interpretation requires standardized measurement conditions.

## 3. Body Size Measurement

### 3.1. Possible measurements

Several measurements can be used to characterize body size in *Drosophila melanogaster*. Each provides different information and has different experimental limitations.

**Body length**

Body length is a linear measurement between two anatomically defined points. It is relatively easy to obtain from calibrated images or microscopy.

Its principal advantage is simplicity. However, the result depends on the anatomical landmarks selected, the orientation of the specimen and the posture of flexible body regions.

It is therefore important to define exactly which points are used when measuring length.

**Thorax length**

Thorax length is measured between defined anatomical landmarks on the thoracic region.

It is frequently used in studies of *Drosophila* morphology and developmental plasticity. It provides a relatively stable measurement because the thorax is more structurally constrained than the abdomen.

Its main limitation is that it represents only one anatomical region. It should therefore be interpreted as an indicator of body size, not as a complete measurement of the organism.

**Body mass**

Body mass measures the quantity of matter in the organism.

It can provide information about overall organismal size, but it is sensitive to hydration, nutritional condition, reproductive state and the amount of material present in the digestive tract.

Dry body mass can reduce some sources of variation associated with water content, although obtaining it requires sacrificing the specimen.

Research on *Drosophila* has used body weight alongside thorax and wing measurements to investigate developmental responses to temperature (Karan et al., 1998).

**Body volume**

Body volume measures the three-dimensional space occupied by the organism.

Unlike length, volume accounts for three spatial dimensions. It may therefore provide a more complete geometrical representation of the body.

However, measuring the volume of a small insect accurately is difficult because its body is not a regular geometric solid. It also contains flexible regions and appendages that complicate the definition of the volume being measured.

### 3.2. Measuring body volume

The total body volume of *Drosophila melanogaster* could theoretically be estimated through three-dimensional imaging or by constructing a geometric approximation from anatomical measurements.

One possible approach would be to divide the main body into three regions: head, thorax and abdomen.

Each region could then be approximated using a simple geometric solid.

For example:

- The head could be approximated by an ellipsoid.
- The thorax could be approximated by an ellipsoid or another suitable three-dimensional shape.
- The abdomen could be represented by a sequence of connected ellipsoidal sections or another approximation that accounts for its segmented structure.

For an ellipsoid, the volume is calculated using:

\[
V = \frac{4}{3}\pi abc
\]

where \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) represent the three semi-axes.

The estimated total volume could then be calculated as:

\[
V_{\text{total}} \approx V_{\text{head}} + V_{\text{thorax}} + V_{\text{abdomen}}
\]

This approach is a geometric approximation rather than a direct biological measurement.

Its accuracy would depend on the quality of the anatomical measurements and how closely the selected geometric shapes represent the real body.

It would also be necessary to define whether wings, legs and other appendages are included in the volume calculation.

Three-dimensional imaging methods, such as micro-computed tomography, could provide more detailed anatomical reconstructions, but they introduce additional experimental and technical complexity.

Therefore, a geometric volume estimate is a possible future extension of the project, not an established measurement method for the initial model.

### 3.3. Proposed measurement for the project

At this stage, the most defensible approach is to distinguish between a primary body-size indicator and additional morphological measurements.

**Provisional primary measurement: thorax length.**

Thorax length is a strong initial candidate because it is anatomically well-defined and has been used in experimental studies investigating body-size variation in *Drosophila melanogaster*.

It is also less affected by short-term physiological changes than abdominal dimensions or body mass.

**Possible secondary measurement: body mass.**

Body mass could be included in a later experimental stage to investigate the relationship between structural size and organismal mass.

**Possible future measurement: estimated body volume.**

A three-dimensional volume estimate could eventually provide a more comprehensive geometrical representation, provided that the model's assumptions are clearly defined and its accuracy can be evaluated.

This decision remains provisional. Before implementation, the selected measurement should be compared against available experimental data and the scientific objectives of the simulator.

## 4. Biological Growth and Development

### 4.1. Developmental stages

*Drosophila melanogaster* undergoes complete metamorphosis, also known as holometabolous development.

Its life cycle contains four principal stages:

**Egg**

Development begins after fertilization, when the embryo develops inside the egg. During this stage, the basic body plan and the initial structures of the organism are established.

**Larva**

After hatching, the organism enters the larval stage. The larva feeds actively and undergoes three larval instars, separated by moults.

Most of the increase in body mass and much of the growth associated with adult body size occur during larval development.

**Pupa**

At the end of larval development, the organism enters the pupal stage. During this period, metamorphosis transforms the larval body into the adult body.

Adult structures, including the wings, legs, eyes and adult thorax, develop through extensive tissue remodelling and differentiation.

**Adult**

The adult fly emerges from the pupal case in a process known as eclosion.

After emergence, the adult undergoes physiological maturation and cuticle hardening. However, the exoskeleton constrains further increases in the dimensions of most adult structures.

Consequently, much of the final structural size of the adult is determined before eclosion.

### 4.2. Mechanisms of growth

Body growth is not simply an increase in the number of cells. It involves several interacting biological mechanisms.

**Cell growth** refers to an increase in the size of individual cells. Cells accumulate proteins, organelles and other cellular components.

**Cell division** increases the number of cells through processes such as mitosis.

**Growth rate** describes how quickly tissues and organs increase in size over time.

**Duration of development** determines how long growth can continue before developmental transitions terminate or substantially alter the growth process.

These mechanisms interact to determine the final dimensions of the organism.

In insects, hormonal regulation is particularly important.

Insulin/insulin-like signalling contributes to the regulation of nutrient-dependent growth. The TOR pathway also participates in cellular responses to nutrient availability and growth regulation.

Ecdysone, a steroid hormone, plays a central role in moulting and metamorphosis. Its changing levels help regulate developmental transitions and the termination of larval growth.

The relationship between insulin signalling, ecdysone and developmental timing is therefore fundamental to understanding how insects reach their final body size (Callier & Nijhout, 2013; Nijhout & Callier, 2015).

These mechanisms will be investigated in greater detail in the separate document `body-size-genes.md`.

### 4.3. Final adult size

Two individuals belonging to the same species can reach different adult sizes because they may experience different genetic and environmental conditions during development.

Genetic differences can influence cellular growth, developmental timing, hormonal regulation and the response to environmental conditions.

Environmental differences can affect nutrient availability, temperature and competition during larval development.

The interaction between these factors can produce differences in adult morphology.

Importantly, adult size is not necessarily determined by one isolated factor. It emerges from the combined effects of several developmental processes.

## 5. Environmental Influence

### 5.1. Temperature

Temperature influences metabolic activity, developmental rate and growth in *Drosophila melanogaster*.

A well-documented pattern in many ectothermic insects is that individuals developing at lower temperatures may reach larger adult sizes than individuals developing at higher temperatures. This pattern is often associated with the temperature-size rule.

However, this relationship should not be treated as a universal linear law.

Research on *Drosophila* has demonstrated that body-size responses to temperature can depend on the temperature range, genetic background, sex and developmental conditions.

For example, French, Feast and Partridge (1998) found that developmental temperature influenced thorax, wing and abdominal dimensions. Their results showed that temperature effects accumulated across development and depended on the developmental stage at which the temperature changed.

Other research has shown that reaction norms can be nonlinear, with body-size traits reaching maximum values at intermediate temperatures rather than continuously increasing as temperature decreases (Karan et al., 1998).

For the simulator, temperature should therefore influence body growth through a biologically plausible response function rather than an assumption that every decrease in temperature produces a fixed increase in size.

### 5.2. Food availability

Food availability is one of the important environmental factors affecting larval development.

Larvae require nutrients to support cellular growth, cell division and the development of adult structures.

Both the quantity and nutritional composition of food can influence final adult size.

Research by Poças, Crosbie and Mirth (2022) found that larval nutrition had a stronger influence on several adult size measurements than adult nutrition. The study also showed that different nutritional restrictions affected body mass and appendage dimensions differently.

This distinction is important because body mass and structural dimensions do not necessarily respond identically to environmental conditions.

For the simulator, food availability should influence larval growth and potentially the final adult phenotype.

The nutritional composition of food could be introduced in a later model version, once the effects of basic food availability have been investigated.

### 5.3. Population density

Population density can influence body size through competition for limited resources.

When many larvae develop in the same food substrate, the available nutrients may become insufficient to support the growth requirements of every individual.

Competition can therefore reduce the quantity of food available per larva and alter developmental conditions.

The consequences may include changes in growth rate, developmental duration, survival and final adult size.

However, population density should not be represented as a direct biological cause of smaller body size without considering its underlying mechanisms.

A more defensible initial model would represent density through its effects on resource availability and competition.

This would allow the simulator to distinguish between the number of individuals and the amount of resources available to them.

### 5.4. Other environmental factors

Several additional environmental variables may influence body size.

**Humidity and water availability** can affect physiological condition and the suitability of the developmental environment.

**Photoperiod** can influence biological rhythms and aspects of reproductive and developmental physiology, although its importance for body-size determination should be established before it is included as a direct model variable.

**Nutritional composition** can influence growth independently of total food quantity. The balance between proteins, carbohydrates and other nutrients may affect developmental outcomes.

**Environmental stress** can alter growth and survival when conditions approach or exceed the organism's physiological limits.

These factors should not all be introduced simultaneously. Their effects should first be investigated separately to avoid making it impossible to identify which environmental variable caused an observed change.

## 6. Genetic Influence

Body size is considered a complex quantitative trait because it can be influenced by variation across multiple genes and by environmental conditions.

Unlike a simple Mendelian trait determined by a single locus with clearly distinguishable phenotypic categories, body size generally varies continuously across individuals.

Several genetic mechanisms contribute to this variation.

**Genetic variation** refers to differences in DNA sequences between individuals. Different genetic variants can influence biological processes involved in growth and development.

**Environmental variation** refers to differences caused by environmental conditions, including temperature, nutrition and developmental stress.

**Genotype–environment interaction** occurs when the phenotypic effect of a genotype depends on the environment in which the organism develops. For example, two genotypes may respond differently to the same temperature change.

**Heritability** describes the proportion of phenotypic variation within a particular population and environment that is statistically associated with genetic variation.

Heritability does not mean that a characteristic is determined exclusively by genes. Its value depends on the population and environmental conditions being studied.

Body size therefore emerges from interactions between genetic variation, developmental processes and environmental conditions.

For the Genome Evolution Lab, this means that body size should not initially be represented as the direct result of a single gene.

Instead, the model should eventually account for multiple genetic contributions and environmental effects.

The specific genes and molecular pathways involved in growth will be documented separately in `body-size-genes.md`.

## 7. Representation in the Genome Evolution Lab

This section translates established biological knowledge into preliminary modeling decisions. The decisions below are hypotheses and design proposals for the simulator, not claims that the model reproduces the complete biology of *Drosophila melanogaster*.

### 7.1. Phenotypic variable

The initial model should represent body size through a measurable structural characteristic rather than an undefined concept of total organism size.

Thorax length is proposed as the initial primary variable because it provides a relatively stable anatomical measurement and is used in experimental studies of *Drosophila*.

The model could represent this variable as a continuous numerical phenotype.

Additional variables, such as body mass or estimated volume, could be introduced later if their biological relevance and measurement methods are sufficiently justified.

### 7.2. Individual variation

Individuals should be capable of displaying different body sizes even when they belong to the same population.

This variation may arise from genetic differences, environmental conditions and developmental variation.

A conceptual representation is:

\[
S = f(G,E,D,\varepsilon)
\]

where:

- \(S\) represents the final body-size phenotype.
- \(G\) represents genetic contributions.
- \(E\) represents environmental conditions.
- \(D\) represents developmental processes.
- \(\varepsilon\) represents residual variation not explicitly represented by the model.

This expression is a conceptual framework, not a calibrated biological equation.

The specific mathematical relationship must be established only after the relevant genetic and experimental evidence has been reviewed.

### 7.3. Environmental influence

Temperature and food availability are proposed as the first environmental variables affecting body size.

Temperature is relevant because developmental temperature can alter growth and final adult dimensions.

Food availability is relevant because larval nutrition has a substantial influence on adult structural size.

Population density may also be included, but preferably through its effects on resource availability and competition rather than as an independent arbitrary size modifier.

The initial experiments should isolate environmental variables so that their individual effects can be studied.

### 7.4. Simplifications

The simulator will necessarily simplify several biological processes.

First, it will not reproduce every cell, tissue or organ. Instead, it will represent selected genetic and developmental mechanisms through mathematical relationships.

Second, it will not initially simulate the complete molecular regulation of growth. Selected pathways may be represented through simplified variables once their roles are sufficiently understood.

Third, environmental effects will initially be represented through a limited number of variables rather than a complete ecological environment.

Fourth, individual developmental variation may be represented through a controlled stochastic component.

These simplifications are acceptable if they are explicitly documented, scientifically motivated and tested through controlled experiments.

The objective is not to reproduce every biological detail but to construct a model capable of investigating specific evolutionary questions.

## 8. Scientific Limitations and Open Questions

Several aspects of body-size biology remain to be investigated before a quantitative model can be defined.

First, the most appropriate primary measurement must be confirmed. Thorax length is a strong initial candidate, but the relationship between thorax length, body mass and total body volume needs to be considered.

Second, the genetic architecture of body size is complex. Although several signalling pathways are known to regulate growth, their effects cannot automatically be translated into numerical allelic effects.

Third, environmental responses are not necessarily linear. Temperature and nutritional conditions may produce nonlinear effects and interactions with genetic background.

Fourth, the relationship between body size and biological fitness is not necessarily positive. Larger individuals are not automatically more successful in survival or reproduction. The consequences depend on the environmental context and the particular fitness component being measured.

Fifth, the model will not initially reproduce the complete developmental process of the fly. It will represent selected outcomes of development rather than simulate every cellular and hormonal event.

The following distinctions must therefore remain explicit:

**Established biological facts**

- Adult *Drosophila melanogaster* has a body organized into head, thorax and abdomen.
- Most substantial growth associated with adult body size occurs before adulthood.
- Insulin signalling, TOR-related nutrient sensing and ecdysone regulation participate in growth and developmental transitions.
- Temperature and nutrition can influence adult size.

**Preliminary model assumptions**

- Thorax length will be the initial primary body-size variable.
- Temperature and food availability will be the first environmental variables considered.
- Genetic contributions will eventually be represented through a simplified quantitative model.
- Residual variation may be represented through a stochastic component.

**Open research questions**

- Which genes and variants have sufficiently strong evidence to be represented?
- What quantitative effects do those variants have on relevant body-size measurements?
- Which temperature-response function is supported by experimental evidence?
- How should larval nutrition and population density be represented?
- Can the selected phenotype be compared against published experimental measurements?

These questions must be addressed before the final mathematical model is calibrated.
